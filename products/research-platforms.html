Research platforms where the stack is earned

Two airframes exist purely to build capability that transfers. Vehicle A is an instrumented 10-inch research quad, cheap to fly and cheap to crash. Vehicle B is a 500 km/h-class platform flown autonomously at 300 km/h. Between them they produce the simulator, the control policies and the test infrastructure that every weapon in the portfolio depends on.

02 · Use cases

What these airframes are for

They are not products. They are the instrument through which the company's capability frontier is advanced, and they are listed in this wiki because every specification on every other product page traces back to a sortie flown on one of them.

  • System identification. Fly a manoeuvre, log it densely, replay the same commands in simulation, and find where the prediction diverges. Correct the actuator curve, the aerodynamics or the inertia — at the term, not at the output.
  • Policy development. Train a direct-actuator control policy against the corrected physics, then fly the run the expert demonstrated and see whether it holds.
  • Hardware qualification. A single sortie returns a motor slew nobody predicted, rotational acceleration that does not match the command, a latency spike on the compute bus, and a battery temperature rising earlier than modelled — four changes to four different systems.
  • Envelope expansion. Each flight campaign, simulator correction and retrain recovers a larger fraction of the airframe's true physical envelope.
03 · Specifics

The loop, in order

Step 01
Expert demonstration
A human pilot flies the real aircraft at its limit. That is the number to reach — the performance ceiling is defined by a person before it is defined by a network.
Step 02
Current simulator
Identified from real flight data, not written from first principles or borrowed from a graphics engine.
Step 03
Reinforcement learning
About one billion steps, petabyte-scale rollouts, heavy domain randomisation.
Step 04
Trained policy on the aircraft
Direct actuator control, running on the vehicle rather than on a ground station.
Step 05
Fly it for real
The policy attempts the run the expert demonstrated.
Step 06
Logs against simulation
Repeat the same commands in simulation. Where does the prediction diverge?
Step 07
Correct the simulator, then repeat
Actuator curve, aerodynamics, inertia. The loop concludes when the autonomous policy matches or exceeds the expert in reality and the simulator can predict the real flight.

A parallel data flywheel feeds the same simulator: organised races and fleet flights put more vehicles at the limit and return limit-flight data at a scale no simulator can invent. Once that pool is large enough, the same logs train the policy directly through behaviour cloning, on top of what reinforcement learning finds in simulation.

04 · Specifics

What actually transfers

The asset

The identification rig, the simulator architecture, and the whole loop from expert demonstration through simulator refinement to training. The network weights are specific to a single airframe and do not transfer. The pipeline does — which is why a new airframe is a new package on the same backbone rather than a new programme.

The outcome is a repeatable procedure that can learn a control policy for any airframe, flying near its physical limit. Vehicle A proved the loop. Vehicle B proved it generalises to a vehicle an order of magnitude faster. Ahuti is the first weapon to consume it, and the terminal phase of Nightshade is the next.

Class C · Maritime · dual-use surface vessel · hull & hardware in development

Piranha USV, strike and surveillance from one hull

A low-observable, attritable unmanned surface vessel built on a common wave-piercing hull for strike and persistent maritime ISR. In its strike role, Piranha is designed to carry a 150–250 kg class shaped-charge warhead over 300–500 km using non-satellite maritime odometry and terminal optical tracking. In its dual-use role, it provides coastal agencies with persistent autonomous patrol without crewed vessels.

The hull and onboard hardware are in build now, with sea trials from the end of 2026. Navigation and control follow the core Apollyon approach: physical references the vessel senses for itself, no dependence on GNSS, and autonomy that runs on board.

Major surface combatants require years to construct and cost thousands of crores. Piranha is targeted under ₹65 lakh at rate, using vacuum resin-infusion tooling in domestic commercial boatyards. Sending sixty-lakh attritable surface craft forces adversaries to expend multi-crore surface-to-air missiles and close-in defensive ordnance, which turns the maritime cost exchange in the attacker's favour.

The Piranha USV is built to strike in disputed littoral waters, island chains and hostile anchorages, where crewed naval vessels cannot operate without excessive risk.

04 · Engineering specifics

Navigation, control and warhead

A · Non-satellite sea-surface navigation

Satellite denial over open water presents unique challenges: unlike land terrain, the open sea has no static topographic features for elevation correlation. The Piranha USV employs a layered navigation stack built specifically for marine environments:

  • CRPA Anti-Jam GNSS: Indigenous multi-element antenna suppresses coastal jammers by >45 dB, maintaining NavIC/GPS lock during initial transit.
  • Hydro-Inertial Dead Reckoning: Tactical IMUs fused with a Doppler Velocity Log (DVL). In coastal water the DVL locks onto the seabed and measures true speed over ground, which removes current drift; beyond its bottom-lock depth it measures speed through the water, and the current becomes an estimated state.
  • Coastline DSMAC: As the vessel nears target archipelagos, ports, or coastlines, high-resolution optical cameras match shoreline profiles, headlands, and navigation marks against pre-loaded commercial satellite maps.
  • Optical Waterline Tracking: In the terminal 3 km run, stabilised daylight/MWIR cameras lock onto the target's hull silhouette at the waterline, which chaff and most decoys do not reproduce.

B · Control: the same method, a marine controller

Piranha is controlled the way the aircraft are, with the physics changed from air to water. A guidance layer turns the route into the heading and speed to hold, a controller holds them, and the waterjets carry out the commands. As on every Apollyon platform, the controller's settings come from a model of the vehicle identified from real runs, not from textbook assumptions, and envelope limits keep demand inside what the hull and jets can actually deliver.

The hard part is the equivalent of flying near the edge of the envelope. A fast hull behaves very differently at low speed, while climbing onto the plane and at full sprint, so the control settings are scheduled across those regimes rather than fixed. The sea adds its own problems. The controller has to ignore the rocking of individual waves and follow only the real motion. It has to hold running trim so the hull stays stable at speed, and it has to keep station on the track despite current and wind pushing the vessel sideways.

  • Speed follows the sea: as the sea state rises, the vessel limits its own speed to stay inside the structural limits of the hull.
  • Sharing the water: in transit and on patrol it keeps clear of other traffic using its radar and cameras, and follows the rules of the road.
  • When the link drops: it falls back to a pre-set safe behaviour. The decision to strike always stays with a human commander.

The hull and hardware are still in build, so these settings will be worked out in sea trials, through the same loop the aircraft use: run the vessel, compare the logs with the simulation, correct the model and the settings, and clear each change in hardware-in-the-loop testing before it goes back on the water. One method, many platforms →

C · Heavy shaped-charge penetrating warhead

Unlike blast-only drone boats that produce superficial topside damage, the Piranha USV integrates a custom-engineered 250–300 kg shaped-charge warhead designed to break a warship's hull:

  • Waterline & Sub-Surface Impact: The vessel trims down immediately prior to impact, detonating against the target's hull right at or below the waterline.
  • Copper Jet Penetration: The precision-machined shaped-charge liner drives a hyper-velocity jet through the hull below the waterline, flooding machinery and magazine spaces.
  • Redundant Fuzing: Redundant contact and delayed-action fuzing ensure lethal detonation even during glancing blows.
05 · Dual use

One hull, two customers

A common composite hull supports two distinct mission configurations. The strike variant houses a shaped-charge warhead (150–250 kg class) and impact fuzing. The surveillance variant replaces high explosives with an electro-optical/infrared mast, maritime search radar, and secure tactical datalinks. Port trusts, coast guards, and littoral security forces gain persistent presence across shipping lanes at a fraction of crewed vessel operating costs. Peacetime orders keep the boatyard tooling and workforce busy, so a surge line for strike craft is ready when it is needed.

Programme

Hull and onboard hardware in development through 2026; sea trials, with radar and imaging sensors, from the end of 2026; serial production from 2028. Hull tooling and the navigation package are common between the strike and surveillance variants, so the peacetime customer pays for the tooling the strike line inherits.

06 · Shared subsystems

Subsystems powering this vessel

Guidance & control

Robust flight control · the method

Piranha runs its own marine autopilot. What it inherits from the aircraft programmes is the method: instrumented trials, a model identified from the logs, and hardware-in-the-loop clearance before each configuration goes on the water.

Navigation

GNSS-denied navigation

Anti-jam CRPA NavIC/GPS, coastline scene matching and hydro-inertial dead reckoning for long sea transits.

Compute

Edge compute

Runs waterline tracking, collision avoidance, the sea-state speed governor and mesh coordination on the mission computer.

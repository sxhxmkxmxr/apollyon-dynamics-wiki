Propulsion and the thrust margin that buys manoeuvre

Propulsion sets the ceiling on every other parameter. It decides how fast a vehicle ingresses, how far it goes, and — the part most often missed — how much energy remains for the terminal manoeuvre after a thousand kilometres of cruise at four and a half thousand metres.

02 · The governing physics

High-altitude thrust derate

Turbine mass airflow, and therefore thrust, declines in proportion to ambient air density. At 4,500 m — the operating floor across Eastern Ladakh and North Sikkim — standard atmospheric density is 0.771 kg/m³, or 62.9% of the sea-level 1.225 kg/m³. A turbine sized with minimal margin at sea level loses roughly 38–40% of its static thrust at plateau altitude.

The design rule

Size the engine for the launch floor, not for sea level. A vehicle correctly engined at sea level is underpowered in the mountains, and the failure is not a slower cruise — it is an inability to climb over ridgelines, to reject mountain turbulence, or to pitch up for a terminal attack after the cruise has consumed its fuel.

Thin air at four to five thousand metres also reduces the lift a given wing produces, which shows up as larger wings, worse terminal manoeuvre margin and shorter effective range than a sea-level brochure figure would suggest. A cheap round designed for the plateau is a different vehicle from a cheap round designed for the western desert. That is a design input, not a footnote.

03 · Sovereignty

Why the engine is built here, and not salvaged

The company explicitly rejects designing around decommissioned civilian business-jet or trainer engines. That approach produced the Ukrainian FP-5 Flamingo, and it was the correct decision for Ukraine: thousands of surplus Soviet AI-25 engines sat in storage, cheap and immediately available, and the requirement was to cross a continental landmass during an invasion.

It does not transfer. Constraint-first engineering has a signature failure mode — the thing that made the design possible eventually becomes the thing that caps it. By 2026 the same surplus stockpile was the binding constraint on Flamingo output, with production near three missiles a day against a target of seven, an in-house turbojet six months into development, and serial manufacture pushed into 2027.

For India specifically, foreign engine salvage provides no sustainable production pipeline, triggers MTCR Category I export exposure through excessive vehicle mass, and diverts investment away from domestic aerospace manufacturing. The whole point of the programme is to establish rate tooling for an Indian engine.

04 · Industrial base

Who builds it

  • GTRE → Azad Engineering. GTRE completed design and initial handover of the 350 kgf expendable turbojet to Azad Engineering on 22 July 2026. Azad holds the multi-axis CNC capacity and dynamic rotor-balancing rigs required for blisks and turbine casings.
  • Godrej Aerospace. Precision modules.
  • Bharat Forge and Aerolloy. Raw superalloy forgings.

At rate, the engine is approximately 28% of flyaway cost — ₹45–50 lakh of a ₹1.5–1.8 crore round. An indigenous 40–100 kgf turbojet family sits below it for the lighter classes.

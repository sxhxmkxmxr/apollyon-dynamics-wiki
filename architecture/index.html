Apollyon Dynamics · Architecture

The shared core, in hardware, software and method

Apollyon structures strike weapon development around a permanent guidance and computational core. Rather than re-engineering flight controllers and navigation filters for every new missile airframe, a unified architecture—state estimation, adaptive guidance laws, deterministic runtimes, and empirical simulation models—serves the entire strike family. Introducing a new vehicle requires structural integration and aerodynamic packaging rather than a multi-year avionics redesign.

This document defines the physical mechanisms, computational pipelines, and mathematical models that govern that shared architecture.

01 · Depth × speed

Where the limit comes from

Commercial supply chains supply individual drone components, but system performance derives from subsystem integration. The operational limits of high-speed uncrewed aircraft—terminal speed, maneuver g-load, and electronic warfare survivability—are determined by battery thermals, bus communication latency, and the physical accuracy of simulation models.

Commercial off-the-shelf autopilots impose conservative software governors and fixed gain schedules that prevent high-rate maneuvering. Purchased airframes constrain aerodynamic and packaging choices, while generic flight simulators fail to capture cross-axis aerodynamic coupling at high dynamic pressures. Apollyon retains sovereign engineering ownership over the critical performance drivers: state estimation algorithms, flight control runtimes, battery discharge dynamics, and mission-matched composite structures. Standard passive components and raw materials are procured across diversified supply lines, as documented in the supply chain review.

Internal ownership enables rapid iteration. Because every control loop, power interface, and estimator parameter is accessible, telemetry from each flight directly updates internal physics models and software binaries.

03 · Software

Core vehicle architecture, software view

A unified codebase operates across all strike systems. Vehicle variations execute as configuration parameters rather than separate code branches, ensuring software optimizations and security patches propagate automatically across the entire fleet.

Fig. 02functional view
Fig. 02 The same stack in software: sensor drivers feed a fused state estimate; guidance and envelope protection constrain the control law; mission and autonomy layer on top; platform services and a real-time foundation underneath.

Three architectural constraints govern this software stack:

First, the state estimator serves as the sole consumer of raw sensor inputs. Guidance algorithms and flight controllers receive a single fused probabilistic state estimate rather than asynchronous sensor streams.

Second, control policies command actuators directly. Rather than tuning layered PID loops, direct-actuator neural network policies are trained directly against empirical vehicle physics, preserving control authority at edge flight envelopes (§05).

Third, mission management separates cleanly from core flight dynamics. Waypoints, digital terrain-following corridors, optical terminal tracking, and geofence abort triggers run as configurable mission scripts within a common runtime, allowing the same guidance stack to direct a 300 km loitering effector or a 1,500 km cruise missile.

04 · Engineering system

The machine, its twin, and the loop

Hardware flight trials represent half of the development process. The remaining half consists of a persistent system identification pipeline: a high-fidelity digital twin calibrated against instrumented flight data, and an automated software update loop that translates flight test data into verified hardware and software revisions.

Fig. 03machine · loop · asset
Fig. 03 The engineering system in three registers: A — the machine (reusable state estimation and control kernel), B — the loop (rapid parameter identification and edge envelope retuning), and C — the compounding asset (cross-generation codebase and test rig reuse across the fleet).

The digital twin and system identification

Control policies depend entirely on the physical fidelity of their training simulation. Conventional simulators model nominal linear aerodynamics, which break down during high-speed, high-incidence flight. Apollyon operates a unified simulation architecture in which individual airframes exist as parameterised configurations rather than separate software codebases. Flight test logs calibrate the model after every campaign: lift and drag polars, control-surface hinge moments, rotational moments of inertia, motor thermal curves, and electrical bus transient drops are resolved to specific physical terms rather than lumped noise parameters.

A secondary online diagnostic network processes live telemetry to detect external disturbances—such as sudden mountain gusts, actuator thermal degradation, and cell voltage dips. This real-time state estimate allows the primary flight controller to compensate immediately for environmental extremes outside nominal pre-flight profiles.

Fig. 04vehicle · twin
Fig. 04 The vehicle and its twin. Empirical flight logs identify aerodynamics and actuator dynamics; the 6-DOF twin synthesizes estimation and control policies; real-time hardware-in-the-loop emulation clears each software revision before airframe flight.

The iteration loop

Instrumented flight sorties collect high-rate telemetry across all structural, electrical, and control channels. Within hours of recovery, data pipelines convert each test into four direct outputs: updated coefficients in the flight dynamics model, verified execution timings for the real-time kernel, retrained control policies for edge envelope boundaries, and structural tolerances for subsequent manufacturing batches.

Consequently, each production standard achieves expanded flight envelopes with greater control authority. Because data ingestion, hardware-in-the-loop test benches, and simulator physics are permanent engineering assets, subsequent vehicle programs achieve validation faster and at lower unit cost.

05 · Control

Control at the edge of the envelope

High-speed tactical flight forces vehicles into non-linear, cross-coupled aerodynamic regimes where classical control theory fails.

At velocities exceeding 80 metres per second, aerodynamic interactions introduce six critical operational boundaries:

  1. Dynamic pressure non-linearities: Aerodynamic drag scales quadratically with velocity, increasing forty-nine-fold between 20 m/s and 140 m/s while varying substantially with ambient air density and altitude.
  2. Cross-axis kinematic coupling: Yaw, pitch, and roll axes become strongly coupled through gyroscopic moments and asymmetrical vortex shedding over control surfaces.
  3. Propulsive efficiency loss: Propeller and turbine efficiency drops sharply as tip speeds approach sonic regimes or when incoming airflow exceeds optimal intake design velocity.
  4. Sensor saturation: High-g maneuvers combined with high-frequency structural vibration saturate standard ±16 g inertial measurement units.
  5. Actuator bandwidth limits: Control servos and speed controllers exhibit non-linear response lag under maximum aerodynamic load.
  6. Control authority exhaustion: When actuators operate near maximum deflection or full electrical throttle, no control headroom remains to reject sudden crosswind gusts.

Traditional cascaded PID loops (outer position loop, inner attitude loop, rate loop, and actuator allocation mixer) presume linear control reserves at each stage. When actuators saturate at maximum velocity, cascaded controllers suffer windup and cross-axis instability. Similarly, classical model-predictive control relies on simplified aerodynamic equations that become inaccurate during flow separation.

Apollyon deploys direct-actuator learned control policies. Neural network controllers evaluate estimated airframe states and path geometry at 500 Hz to generate direct surface and thrust commands. Because actuator lag, surface saturation, and high-dynamic-pressure turbulence are modeled directly within domain-randomized training simulations, the flight software maintains stability throughout extreme maneuvers.

A secondary world model network runs concurrently as a diagnostic observer. By monitoring high-frequency deviations between expected acceleration and observed inertial telemetry, the world model isolates motor degradation, control surface icing, or severe wind shears, adjusting controller gains before boundary limits are violated.

Fig. 05convergence · transfer
Fig. 05 The simulator is earned one real flight at a time. A human expert defines the performance ceiling; every autonomous flight iteratively refines the physics until the policy matches or exceeds the expert and the simulator predicts the real flight. The optimization loop, in sequence

Operational flight envelopes are bounded through instrumented flight trials → flight dynamics models are identified directly from recorded sensor logs → reinforcement learning trains control policies across approximately 109 domain-randomized simulated time-steps → compiled policy binaries execute on hardware-in-the-loop test benches → validated binaries deploy to the aircraft for envelope expansion flights → telemetry is replayed in simulation to correct residual discrepancies. Fleet operational hours continuously populate empirical limit datasets, providing training distributions that synthetic modeling cannot replicate.

06 · Compute

At a hundred metres per second, latency is a distance

At 100 metres per second, a 15-millisecond computational delay displaces an airframe by 1.5 metres before a control surface begins moving. In high-speed intercept or low-altitude terrain following, processing latency translates directly into geometric tracking error.

Fig. 06worst case, not average
Fig. 06 Distance flown while one control cycle runs. The conventional stack spends 1.9 m in copies through user space, runtime overhead, inference, actuate and jitter; Apollyon's execution path completes within 0.25 m, converting saved compute time into reaction margin.

Apollyon’s onboard flight compute stack addresses latency through three architectural constraints:

First, fused execution kernels execute neural inference and state estimation without dynamic memory allocation or thread handover delays.

Second, a hard real-time kernel assigns dedicated CPU cores to high-rate sensor sampling and control computation, preventing system interrupts from delaying the 500 Hz inner loop.

Third, a zero-copy data path streams digitized sensor frames and IMU registers directly into shared inference memory via direct memory access (DMA), eliminating user-space memory copies.

08 · Navigation

The satellite signal is the most unreliable signal in defence

It arrives weak from a 20,000 km orbit, and an inexpensive ground jammer drowns it across tens of kilometres. Spoofers broadcast forged constellations and standard receivers cannot tell they are being deceived. Degradation arrives before denial: multipath and partial jamming introduce errors of hundreds of metres while the receiver still reports a healthy link.

Every remaining reference is sensed by the aircraft

Onboard sensors do not measure absolute coordinates — they measure immediate surroundings, and a true position emerges only when those measurements are matched against maps stored before flight. The stack degrades gracefully across five references:

  1. CRPA antenna — spatial null-steering that suppresses ground jammers across bands.
  2. Multi-band, multi-constellation GNSS — GPS, GLONASS, Galileo and NavIC in parallel, the primary position source when it is available.
  3. Inertial bridge — tactical MEMS/FOG inertial navigation that carries attitude and position through dropouts. Grade buys time, not a bound: better inertial sensors drift more slowly, never less. Spending delays drift, it never stops it.
  4. Terrain-referenced and scene-matched position — the shape of the ground (ranging, matched against stored elevation) and the look of the ground (a camera frame, matched against stored imagery). Both are carried onboard, sized to the mission; nothing is transmitted while flying, so there is nothing to jam and nothing to spoof.
  5. Terminal seeker — EO/IR lock for the last kilometres, independent of every satellite and of RF.

Matching that survives the seasons

Classical scene matching compared raw brightness, so winter, snow, changing sun angle or a thermal camera broke the lock. Apollyon's approach uses learned descriptions: models pre-trained on planetary-scale Earth imagery that map terrain into latent embeddings of permanent structure — road networks, ridgelines, forest boundaries, building footprints — while filtering transient noise like snow, shadow and foliage. The result is that years-old public satellite imagery can be matched against a live operational camera feed, with the model returning both a match and a confidence that lets the estimator weight or refuse it. The reference data is public or commercial; no classified imagery of a particular target is required, and every sortie adds to what the next model learns from.

Route planning becomes a navigation decision

Because fixes are geographic, the flight planner plans toward information. Waypoints are selected for navigational contrast; adding a one-minute detour over distinct ridgelines does more to eliminate drift than a million-dollar sensor upgrade. Before crossing featureless expanses the planner forces high-confidence fixes at the boundary, pinning uncertainty to its minimum before the blind stretch — and if accumulated drift approaches mission limits, the aircraft acts on its own: expanding sensor search radii, banking toward verified ground features, or aborting.
